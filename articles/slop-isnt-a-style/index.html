Slop Isn't a Style. It's an Intention.

Everything is slop now. Content slop. Music slop. Art slop. Movie slop. Even food gets called slop. The word showed up a while ago and quickly ate the whole internet. Now it's the default reply to anything someone doesn't like. I've spent 15 years as an art director, and now I make art with my hands and with vibe coding. So I care about this word more than most. When a word means everything, it stops meaning anything. Here's what I think slop actually is, and what it isn't.

1. What slop really means

Before it was a meme, slop had a clear meaning. Something made with minimal effort, mass produced, built to profit from the people consuming it, without caring whether they actually get anything from it. Think of the food that keeps you alive but nobody expects you to enjoy. Bare minimum. Endlessly repeated. Indifferent to the person on the other end. That's slop.

2. Bad is not slop

A film with a huge budget and a broken story is bad. It isn't slop. An ambitious painting that doesn't quite land is a failure. It isn't slop. Failure means someone reached for something and missed. Slop means nobody reached at all. I'll take an honest failure over a perfectly safe nothing every time.

3. “Slop” has become a lazy review

Scroll through any comment section. Something new, something different, something you just didn't vibe with: slop. Different shapes, one stamp. And here's the irony: calling everything slop is itself the lowest effort take you can give. It skips the thinking. It skips the looking. It's a one word verdict on work you didn't actually look at. If you don't like something, say why. That's the difference between criticism and noise.

4. Copying vs. adding

Taking something that already exists isn't automatically slop. Every artist builds on what came before. The question is simple: what did you add? Repost it with a new caption and you've added nothing. Retrace it in a different color and you've added nothing. Bring your eye, your story, your obsession, a new way of seeing, and now it's yours. It's like copying homework and changing a few words. You're still not adding anything to what already exists.

5. Everything has an audience

Not everything that isn't for you is slop. Good work is usually made for someone specific. Cast a real net and you catch twenty people out of a hundred, and those twenty truly get it. Try to please all hundred and you have to sand off every edge until there's no personality left. Made for everyone usually means made for no one. If a piece doesn't speak to you, maybe it wasn't talking to you.

6. The art director's eye

Here's where I'll be honest. I walk into a café and see a menu, a poster, a flyer, and I can tell in a second it was generated and shipped without a single human decision. The same faces. The same fonts. The same perfect, empty balance. Nothing is wrong with it. And nothing is right with it either. It's a layout with no one behind it. That's the real AI slop. Not the tool, but the absence of a person making choices.

7. Yes, I use AI. Just not the way most people do.

I'd be lying if I pretended otherwise. AI is in my studio every day. But most people use it to skip the work: type a prompt, get a finished image, post it, move on. I use it to do more of the work, not less. For me, AI is a teacher. When I wanted my paintings to move, loop and respond, I didn't know how to build that. So I vibe coded, broke things, asked why they broke, and tried again. Every interactive piece taught me something I couldn't do the week before. It's also a sparring partner. I test ideas against it, argue with it, ask it why something works and why something doesn't. I come out of those conversations knowing more, not depending more. What it never gets is the source. The idea is mine. The painting is mine. The meaning, the struggle, the reason a wall keeps falling in an endless loop, all of that comes from a human who cares about it. Most people use AI to replace their growth. I use it to speed mine up. A thread can pass through a machine and still be the same thread. The moment the tool starts making the decisions instead of you, you've stopped making art and started making output.

8. The real test

So what is slop, really? It's not the format. It's not the tool. It's not the budget. It's not whether you personally like it. It's one question: did the maker care how this would land on the other side? If the answer is no, if it was made only to extract a click, a sale, or a minute of your attention, that's slop. However polished it looks. If the answer is yes, it might be rough, strange, or not for you. But it isn't slop.

9. They'll call it slop anyway

Here's what I've learned from putting work online: Someone will call it slop. No matter how careful, how personal, or how slow it was. Some people just like disliking things. You can't control the word they choose. You can only control whether it's true. Make sure it isn't. Then let them say whatever they want.

Make what you'd want to receive

The opposite of slop isn't perfection. It's attention. A crooked line someone actually meant beats a flawless one nobody chose. So before you post, before you ship, before you call someone else's work slop, ask yourself one thing: Would I want to receive this? If yes, make it. If no, make it better. What's something people call slop that you think isn't? Or the other way around?

    About

    YEKTA

    YEKTA is the name Hossein Yektapour paints under. A digital painter, he spent fifteen years as an art director making images for others before turning to his own.

    The work is conceptual and quiet: bodies that come apart, faces that turn into objects, and a red thread that keeps returning. It is the line that ties a thought to what it costs.

    overthink(paint)ing is how he names the process: thinking a feeling all the way through, until it has no choice but to become a picture.

    Alongside painting he makes interactive and generative work.
