The Hours Nobody Applauds.

A whole afternoon lying around. Reading. Listening to music. Staring at a wall. To most people, that looks like wasted time. To an artist, it's often where the work actually begins. Nobody claps for those hours. There's nothing to post. Nothing to show. But almost everything I make started in one of them. Here's what I've come to believe about the work behind the work.

1. Dead time isn't dead

What looks like boredom from the outside is rarely boredom on the inside. It's the space where ideas have room to arrive. Where two unrelated thoughts finally bump into each other. A calendar packed from morning to night leaves no door open for anything new to walk in. The empty hours aren't the opposite of the work. They're where it starts.

2. Nobody will give you that time

No one rewards dead time. Not your family, not your boss, not the feed. The question is always the same: What do you have to show for yourself? So you have to carve it out yourself, on purpose, and protect it like a meeting you can't miss. Curating your own life is part of the creative job.

3. You can't count on lightning

Waiting for inspiration is an amateur's plan. Anyone can have one great night. The professional is the one who can reach that place again, and again, and again. And the night you think was brilliant and the night you think was terrible? Look at the work a week later. The difference is usually smaller than it felt. Lightning in a bottle is luck. A routine that keeps catching it is craft.

4. Prepare more than seems necessary

Fifteen years in advertising taught me that the shoot is not where the idea gets solved. It gets solved before. In the questions you answer in advance. In the problems you see coming. The real nightmare isn't failing. It's showing up to the moment unprepared, with everyone looking at you, and no answers. Some minds are fast and spontaneous. Others are slow and methodical, and need to think things through before they can say them well. Whatever kind of mind you have, the work deserves your preparation.

5. Learn from everyone. Be no one's disciple.

There's no single right way to make art. There are as many ways as there are people making it. That's exactly why you shouldn't hand your whole mind to one teacher, one method, one guru. Take something from everyone. Then let it pass through you and come out as something only you could make. Following one person completely means outsourcing your own wisdom.

6. You're surrounded by mentors

A lot of people wait for a mentor to find them. But every painter, writer and thinker who ever left a record is available to you, right now. They put everything they had into that work. It's a concentrated version of a life, waiting to be opened. I don't always remember the exact lines or the exact brushstrokes. But I remember the essence, and it sticks to me. You don't need a mentor who knows you. You need the discipline to sit with the ones who left something behind.

7. Learn to like your own mind

We hear a lot about liking ourselves. Usually it's about how we look. But for an artist, the more important question is: Do I enjoy my own head space? Your interests. Your strange connections. The flow of your thoughts when nobody's watching. Hours alone in front of a blank canvas are only bearable if you actually like the company. The most important relationship in a studio is the one you have with your own mind.

8. Your skills need time to catch up with your ideas

In the beginning, your ideas are always ahead of your hands. I feel that again right now. With interactive pieces and vibe coding, what I can imagine is far ahead of what I can build. Every piece closes the gap a little. So I need two kinds of patience at once. Impatience with the work: push every day, as hard as I can. Patience with the world: don't expect anyone to notice for a while. Many artists come into their own late. That isn't failure. It's just the length of the road.

9. A day is a big thing

We treat a day as small. Something to get through. But a day is enormous. A few hours of painting, a few hours of study, a film, a book, a walk. Write a reasonable list, and you'll often finish it, and then some. You can move a whole practice forward in a single day. Stop treating days like they're cheap.

10. Good leaders don't break people

As an art director, I learned that not everyone needs the same thing. Some people need space. Some need encouragement. Some need a push. A good leader can tell the difference. The worst kind of leadership treats everyone like a recruit to be broken down and rebuilt. It assumes nobody wants to do good work unless you shout at them. Most people already want to do their best. They just need respect, and a clear picture of the goal. Leading creative people means getting out of the way of what they already are.

Give more than you take

I believe the world mostly gives back what you put into it. If you move through it trying to extract, manipulate and take, you might succeed. You probably won't be very happy. If you move through it trying to give, the work, the people and the opportunities around you start to reflect that. And sometimes the simplest thing you can give costs nothing. One honest, encouraging sentence to someone just starting out. It doesn't take much to encourage an artist. One good word can carry someone for years. Where do your best ideas actually come from?

    About

    YEKTA

    YEKTA is the name Hossein Yektapour paints under. A digital painter, he spent fifteen years as an art director making images for others before turning to his own.

    The work is conceptual and quiet: bodies that come apart, faces that turn into objects, and a red thread that keeps returning. It is the line that ties a thought to what it costs.

    overthink(paint)ing is how he names the process: thinking a feeling all the way through, until it has no choice but to become a picture.

    Alongside painting he makes interactive and generative work.
