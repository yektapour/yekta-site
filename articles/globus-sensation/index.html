Globus Sensation: A Painting That Screams When You Touch It

Globus Sensation is a painting of a man melting into the corner of a room. Behind him, on the wall, there are faces. When you first open the piece, the wall is empty. Touch the man, and the face closest to where you touched bursts onto the wall in blood. The scream comes a moment later, from somewhere far away, and then the blood slowly runs down the wall and fades. The whole time, he keeps breathing, heavy and wet. This is how it was made, from the first sketch to the final file.

The sketch

It started as a line drawing: the man sitting slumped in the corner, his head dropped to one side, and the faces around him on the wall. The faces were drawn as studies of one thing, a scream, each from a different angle and with a different weight. The corner of the room is just two lines. It's the only architecture in the picture and it gives him nowhere to go.

Blocking in

Next came the body, blocked in with broad, loose strokes. The faces stayed as pencil lines while the figure took on mass and colour. Warm, raw flesh against the flat grey of the wall. The first drips already show up here, on the shoulder and the chest.

The melt

In the final painting the body is coming apart. Skin runs off his shoulders and knees in thick, waxy drips and collects into a pool on the floor around him. The faces were repainted in dark, smeared red, loose and violent next to the slow, heavy melt of the body. One decision in the painting made everything after it possible: every face was painted on its own layer. The final PSD has seven layers: the painting itself, five separate faces and the torn paper frame.

Making it alive

I built the interactive version together with an AI coding assistant (Claude). The rule from the start was that it should never feel like an effect laid on top of a painting. Everything that moves had to come out of the painting itself. To make him breathe, the man had to be lifted off the wall. A segmentation network cut him out together with the pool, and the edges were then refined against the original brushwork so the outline stays faithful to the paint. The wall behind him was rebuilt, so when his body moves there is wall underneath it and not a hole.

Breathing

Only his torso, shoulders and head breathe; the legs and the pool stay still. Each breath is slightly different from the last: a slow, uneven inhale that catches a few times on the way in, a short hold, then a long, heavy exhale. After every scream his breathing gets faster for a while and then settles down again.

The touch map

The body is divided into five zones, each one wired to the nearest face on the wall. Touch his head and one of the faces above him appears. Touch his knee and it's the one on the right; his arm, the one on the left; his foot, the one at the bottom right. There are no buttons and no instructions. The body is the interface.

The blood

The splash is simulated in real time on the GPU, and it looks different every time. Each burst is made of several hundred droplets: a few heavy drops, many small ones, thin streaks shooting out from the edges, and a fine mist. They fly outward and stretch in the direction of travel, so the spatter always points away from the body. Each face bursts outward from its centre as though it had just hit the wall.

The surface of the blood is shaded like a real liquid film. Light passes through it and gets absorbed, so thin edges glow bright red and thick pools go almost black. While it's wet, it shines. Then gravity takes over. Blood runs down from the heaviest parts of each face in narrow drips with a bead at the end. They speed up, stick, slip and slow down again, the way real drips move on a rough wall. For about fifteen seconds after a splash, new drips keep breaking out of the face. The shine goes after roughly ten seconds as the blood dries dark, and the face slowly dissolves, until a minute later the wall is empty again.

Sound

No sound in the piece is recorded. All of it is synthesized live in the browser, so no two breaths, splashes or screams are exactly the same. Breath: filtered airflow shaped like a throat, with a narrow wheeze and a wet rattle. Splash: the impact, a wet slap, then a scatter of tiny droplet sounds. Scream: a synthesized voice. Each of the five faces has its own voice, sent through a long reverb and echo so it sounds like it's coming from far away. Under everything there's an almost silent room tone, so the space never goes completely dead.

One file

The whole piece is a single HTML file of about 3 MB: the painting, the simulation and all the sound. It runs offline, on desktop and on phones, with no server behind it.

The loop

Without the viewer the piece stays quiet: a man breathing in an empty corner. The faces only appear because you touched him. Every scream is something you caused, and every one fades away until the next touch.

    About

    YEKTA

    YEKTA is the name Hossein Yektapour paints under. A digital painter, he spent fifteen years as an art director making images for others before turning to his own.

    The work is conceptual and quiet: bodies that come apart, faces that turn into objects, and a red thread that keeps returning. It is the line that ties a thought to what it costs.

    overthink(paint)ing is how he names the process: thinking a feeling all the way through, until it has no choice but to become a picture.

    Alongside painting he makes interactive and generative work.
