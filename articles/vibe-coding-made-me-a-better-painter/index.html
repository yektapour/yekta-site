Vibe Coding Made Me a Better Painter.

I'm a painter. Every one of my paintings is made by hand, stroke by stroke. A while ago I wanted something my paintings couldn't do on their own. I wanted them to move. To loop. To respond when someone touched them. I'm not a programmer. So I learned to build them through vibe coding, one broken attempt at a time. I expected it to teach me about code. I didn't expect it to change the way I paint.

1. I went in as a painter, not a programmer

I want to be honest about this part. I don't sit and write code line by line. I describe what I see, test it, break it, ask why it broke, and try again. The brush is still mine. The idea is still mine. The meaning is still mine. Vibe coding is just the bridge that lets a still image cross over into time. And crossing that bridge made me look back at the painting from the other side.

2. A painting stopped being one image

When I made Futility, a figure pushing a cube at a triangular hole in a wall, I couldn't paint it once. I had to paint the figure twice: at rest, and at the moment of impact. I had to paint the wall five times, each with a deeper crack. Suddenly the question wasn't what does this moment look like? It was: what happens just before it, and just after? Now I ask that even when a painting will never move. Every still image is one frame of something larger. Painting it that way makes it feel alive.

3. Layers became decisions

Every digital painter works in layers. For years, I used them mostly as a technical convenience. Interactive work doesn't allow that. Background, figure, wall, debris, each one has to stand on its own, because each one will move on its own. So I had to decide, clearly, what belongs to what. That clarity came back into my still paintings. Better separation of planes. Cleaner depth. Fewer muddy areas where things blur together by accident.

4. Painting the before, not just the after

In Globus Sensation, the wall starts almost clean, with only small stains. Touch the figure, and blood red screaming faces burst across the wall, drip down, and slowly fade. To build that, I had to paint the calm version of the room as carefully as the violent one. I learned that the quiet state isn't empty. It's tension, waiting. Now I spend more time on what a painting is holding back.

5. Time entered the canvas

A loop changes everything you think about an ending. In Futility, the wall cracks, falls, the pieces fade on the floor, and an identical wall appears behind it. Forever. The meaning isn't in any single frame. It's in the repetition. Once you've built something whose meaning lives in time, you start feeling time inside still images too. The weight of what just happened. The pressure of what's about to.

6. Composing for a hand, not just an eye

In a still painting, you guide the eye. In an interactive one, you also have to guide the hand. Where will someone touch first? What should happen when they do? What will they miss if they don't? That forced me to think harder about the focal point than any design rule ever did. If the viewer's finger doesn't know where to go, their eye doesn't either.

7. Seeing an image as a signal

An Image, Listened To turns a picture into sound. Every feature of the image shapes what you hear. Building it meant reading images in a way I never had: brightness, contrast, color, density, rhythm, as information that has to sound like something. A flat, low contrast area sounds dull. A sharp edge sounds like a strike. I now hear my paintings a little before I see them. Where they're too quiet. Where they're too loud. Where nothing is happening at all.

8. Learning what not to move

The first instinct with interactive art is to animate everything. It always looks cheap. In Futility, only the wall with the hole falls. Nothing else. I wanted it to feel natural and real, not like a toy. Deciding what stays still turned out to be as important as deciding what moves. Same in painting. Not every corner needs detail. Not every area needs to shout. Restraint is what lets the important part speak.

9. Breaking things made me braver

Vibe coding is mostly failure. Something doesn't work. You find out why. You try again. After enough of that, failure stops feeling like a verdict. It's just information. That changed how I treat a painting that isn't working. I used to protect a piece once it was halfway good. Now I'm more willing to break it, repaint a whole area, try the risky version. Nothing is so precious it can't be rebuilt.

A new tool, a new pair of eyes

I went looking for a way to make my paintings move. I came back with a different way of seeing the ones that don't. Every still painting now feels like one chosen frame in a longer story, and I paint it knowing what comes before and after. That's the strange gift of learning something new: it rarely stays in its own lane. It walks back into everything you already know, and rearranges it. The brush is still the brush. I just hold it differently now. Has a new tool ever changed how you see your old one?

    About

    YEKTA

    YEKTA is the name Hossein Yektapour paints under. A digital painter, he spent fifteen years as an art director making images for others before turning to his own.

    The work is conceptual and quiet: bodies that come apart, faces that turn into objects, and a red thread that keeps returning. It is the line that ties a thought to what it costs.

    overthink(paint)ing is how he names the process: thinking a feeling all the way through, until it has no choice but to become a picture.

    Alongside painting he makes interactive and generative work.
