Every Painting Is a Self Portrait.

People sometimes look at one of my paintings and ask: Is that you? The man leaning against the wall. The figure with branches growing from her head. The one pushing a cube into a hole that will never fit. The honest answer is no. And also, completely, yes.

1. No face, still a portrait

I almost never paint my own face. But I've come to believe it doesn't matter. Every painting is a self portrait, whether the artist appears in it or not. Not because of who is in the picture, but because of who decided everything about it.

2. Every choice is a fingerprint

Which subject. Which colors. Where the light falls. What's sharp and what's lost in shadow. What stays in the frame and what gets cut. None of those decisions are neutral. Each one comes from somewhere inside you: your history, your fears, your taste, what you can't stop thinking about. Put enough of those decisions together, and you've drawn a map of a person. Even if that person never appears.

3. You paint what you notice

The world offers everyone the same thousands of things. An artist only paints a few of them. What you notice, what stops you, what you return to, says more about you than any face could. A self portrait isn't a picture of what you look like. It's a picture of what you look at.

4. The wall that keeps coming back

In Futility, a figure pushes a cube against a triangular hole in a wall. The wall cracks and falls. An identical wall appears behind it. The cube never fits. Forever. I didn't set out to paint myself. But I know that feeling. Pushing at something that doesn't give. Breaking through one wall only to meet the next. Most people who stop in front of it know that feeling too. That's the strange thing: the more personal a painting is, the more people find themselves in it.

5. The fear behind the flesh

In Drained Flesh, a heavy, exhausted man leans against a wall beside smooth, empty mannequins. It's a painting about how a human being can slowly become something unrecognizable. You don't paint a fear like that from the outside. Somewhere, it's a question I ask about myself. About all of us. How much of us is still here.

6. The parts of you that you'd rather not show

Not every self portrait is flattering. Some paintings carry the parts of you that you would never put on a profile: doubt, exhaustion, the darker corners of being human. That's exactly why they matter. A painting can say what you can't say out loud, and still let you hide behind the brush.

7. And the parts that hope

In Ameretat, after a forest fire, a figure turns her hair into branches so the birds can build nests again. That painting is a self portrait too. Just of a different side. The side that believes in rebuilding. The side that wants to protect something fragile. You're not one person in your work. You're all of them, one painting at a time.

8. Over time, the portrait gets clearer

One painting shows a fragment of you. Ten paintings show a pattern. A whole body of work starts to show a face, one the artist often recognizes last. Looking back at my own work, I see things I never planned: bodies under pressure, transformation, endless effort, small acts of protection.

9. Your obsessions give you away

Some things keep returning, without being invited. For me, a red thread. It runs through my paintings, through my website, through work that has nothing else in common. I could explain it. But part of me thinks it explains me better than I could. The symbols you can't stop using are the closest thing you have to a signature.

10. You can't paint someone else's life

Some artists try to avoid all this. They follow trends, copy styles, paint whatever performs. But even that is a self portrait. It shows what they're afraid of: being seen. You can't escape being in your work. You can only decide whether it's the real you, or a version you're hiding behind.

11. The mask always slips

Every artist wears a mask sometimes. A style. A safe subject. A clever surface. But look long enough at any body of work, and the mask slips. The choices give it away. The hesitations. The places where the brush was sure, and the places where it wasn't. The work always knows more about the artist than the artist is willing to say.

12. It's the viewer's portrait too

Here's the part I love most. When someone stands in front of a painting, they don't just see the artist. They see themselves. The man against the wall becomes their exhaustion. The wall that never ends becomes their struggle. A painting is a mirror with two sides. One faces the artist. The other faces whoever is looking.

So make it honest

If every painting is going to be a self portrait anyway, there's only one real choice left: Make it a true one. Not the version of you that performs well. Not the one you think people want. The one that's actually there, with all its fear, hope, contradiction and stubbornness. That's the only self portrait worth hanging. Where do you see yourself in work you didn't mean to make about you?

YEKTA.

    About

    YEKTA

    YEKTA is the name Hossein Yektapour paints under. A digital painter, he spent fifteen years as an art director making images for others before turning to his own.

    The work is conceptual and quiet: bodies that come apart, faces that turn into objects, and a red thread that keeps returning. It is the line that ties a thought to what it costs.

    overthink(paint)ing is how he names the process: thinking a feeling all the way through, until it has no choice but to become a picture.

    Alongside painting he makes interactive and generative work.
