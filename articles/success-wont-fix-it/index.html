Success Won't Fix It.

Most of us carry a quiet promise around with us. When the work finally takes off, I'll feel different. When the right person notices. When the name travels. When the piece lands in the right place. I want those things too. I'm not pretending otherwise. But the longer I make work, the more I think that promise is a trap. Here's why.

1. The hole doesn't fill from outside

We think outward success will change something inside us. It can make life more comfortable. It can open doors. It doesn't change who you are. Whatever empty space you hoped it would fill stays exactly where it was. Some of the most celebrated artists have been deeply unhappy at the peak of their success. Not because something went wrong. Because the thing that was supposed to fix everything didn't. Success is a result. It's not a cure.

2. Don't spend twenty years waiting to feel better

Imagine working twenty years toward one goal, with no joy along the way, because the goal will make it worth it. Then you reach it. And nothing changes. That's where hopelessness comes from. Not from failing, but from arriving and finding nothing there. If the road isn't worth walking, the destination won't save it. Do the work because it's interesting to you. Follow what pulls you. The outcome was never yours to predict anyway.

3. Criticism tells you about the critic

Every piece I make passes through my filter: my history, my eye, my obsessions. Then it reaches you, and passes through yours. So even if we both love the same painting, we probably don't love it the same way, or for the same reasons. That's why a harsh comment often says as much about the person writing it as about the work. The strongest artists can read a terrible review and laugh. Not because they don't care, but because they know whose filter they're looking through.

4. The only real competition is you

Awards, rankings, “best of the year” lists: they compare things that can't really be compared. Apples and oranges, with a trophy. The only honest comparison is with yourself. Is this the best I can make today? Did I go further than last time? That's the only race that makes sense, and the only one you can actually win.

5. Success has its own trap

When something works, the temptation is obvious: make more of it. Maybe you get away with it once more. By the third time it looks the same, it has stopped being interesting, to the audience and to you. The danger isn't failure. It's getting comfortable with what already worked. The artists who last keep pulling the thread further, even when the old version is still paying off.

6. Your job doesn't have to be your purpose

For about fifteen years, advertising was my job. There's a romantic idea that if you're a real artist, your art has to pay for everything. I don't buy it. Your job can be your job. Your purpose can live in the rest of your waking hours. A job that supports you isn't a betrayal of the dream. It's the floor the dream stands on. Supporting yourself is a noble thing. Don't let chasing the dream destroy your ability to do it.

7. Pressure to pay the rent changes the work

Put all your eggs in the art basket, with rent due, and something shifts. You start making what might sell. What might perform. What's safe. That pressure doesn't make you a better artist. It quietly bends you into a different one. Stability isn't the enemy of freedom. Often it's what makes freedom possible.

8. There is no Mount Olympus

There's a myth that the people who make what we love are a different species. Geniuses, living on a mountain above the rest of us. They aren't. They're people, doing their best. Good at some things, bad at others. Sometimes they find a way to make something beautiful. So can you. The pedestal is empty. It always was.

9. Teach by how you work

People don't like being told what to do. The best way to inspire someone is to let them see how you carry yourself. How you show up. How you treat the work. It's hard to teach something you don't practice. Make things with care, in public, consistently. Someone is watching who needed to see that it's possible.

Know that you don't know

The wisest thing an artist can know is how little they know. Hold your ideas loosely. Maybe that's true. Maybe it could work. Who knows? Listen to the small intuitions: take the other street, try the other color, follow the strange idea that doesn't make sense yet. The moment you're sure you know how it all works, your world gets a lot smaller. Keep it big. Keep making. Let success come or not. It was never going to fix it anyway. What are you waiting for success to fix?

    About

    YEKTA

    YEKTA is the name Hossein Yektapour paints under. A digital painter, he spent fifteen years as an art director making images for others before turning to his own.

    The work is conceptual and quiet: bodies that come apart, faces that turn into objects, and a red thread that keeps returning. It is the line that ties a thought to what it costs.

    overthink(paint)ing is how he names the process: thinking a feeling all the way through, until it has no choice but to become a picture.

    Alongside painting he makes interactive and generative work.
