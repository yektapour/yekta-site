You Become What You Look At.

Every artist is a filter. Everything you see, read, hear and live goes in. Something comes out on the canvas. We spend a lot of time talking about the output: technique, style, consistency, posting. We talk far less about the input. But the quality of what comes out of you can never rise much higher than the quality of what you let in.

1. What goes in comes out

Nothing in your work comes from nowhere. The colors you reach for, the compositions that feel right, the ideas that excite you, all of it is built from what you've absorbed over years. Feed your eyes great work, and your hand slowly learns what great feels like. Feed them noise, and that's what your hand learns too. You don't just make art. You digest the world, and the art is what's left.

2. Taste is built, layer by layer

Taste isn't a gift some people are born with. It's a sediment. Every good painting you study, every great film, every well made thing leaves a thin layer behind. Over years, those layers become the ground you stand on when you judge your own work. That's why your taste usually runs ahead of your skill. It's been fed longer. If you want better taste, there's no shortcut. You have to look at better things, for longer.

3. The feed is a diet

Most of what we see now is chosen for us. An algorithm doesn't care whether something is great. It cares whether you'll keep scrolling. So the feed drifts toward the average. The familiar. The same trend in a hundred slightly different versions. Hours of that every day doesn't feel like anything. But it quietly sets your idea of what “normal” looks like. You wouldn't eat whatever was thrown at you all day. Don't let your eyes do it either.

4. Look at great work, and look slowly

Scrolling past a masterpiece in half a second isn't seeing it. Stand in front of a great painting long enough and it starts to show you things. How the light is built. Why that edge is soft and that one is hard. Where the painter hesitated, and where they were sure. As an art director, I learned that the difference between good and great usually lives in details most people never notice. The only way to start noticing them is to give great work more time than feels necessary.

5. Look outside your own field

If you only look at paintings, you'll eventually paint like everyone else who only looks at paintings. Architecture can teach you structure. Music can teach you rhythm and silence. Film can teach you timing. Nature can teach you everything. The most surprising ideas usually come from places your field isn't looking. Bring something into your work that didn't come from your work.

6. Read. Your concepts depend on it.

For a conceptual artist, images aren't enough. The ideas behind the work come from somewhere. Philosophy. Literature. History. Science. A single sentence in a book can become a whole series. Shallow reading leads to shallow concepts. Thin ideas with a beautiful surface. A painting can only be as deep as the thinking behind it.

7. The people around you set your level

This goes beyond what you look at. It's also who you spend time with. Be around people who are better than you. People who think more deeply, work harder, see further. It's uncomfortable at first. You feel behind. That feeling is exactly the point. Their standards slowly become your standards. And the reverse is just as true. Spend your days with people who settle for little, and little starts to feel like enough.

8. The shallow leaks into the hand

Here's the part nobody likes to hear. Low quality input doesn't just waste time. It changes you. Endless recycled trends, cheap outrage, content made to be consumed in three seconds: it lowers your sense of what's worth making. You start chasing quick reactions instead of lasting images. Your eye gets lazy. Your ideas get thinner. You don't notice it happening. You only notice, months later, that your work feels emptier and you're not sure why.

9. What you pin becomes what you make

Every art director knows the moodboard. Before a campaign exists, you collect references. And whatever goes on that board shapes everything that follows. Pin mediocre references, and you'll get a mediocre campaign, no matter how talented the team is. Your life is a moodboard too. Everything you look at every day is a reference you didn't know you were collecting. Choose the references on purpose.

10. Curate the space you work in

What hangs on your walls, what's on your desk, what's playing while you paint, all of it is input. You see it a hundred times a day. Some part of you is always absorbing it. A studio full of clutter and noise trains your eye one way. A space with a few things you deeply admire trains it another. Design your surroundings as carefully as you'd design a composition.

11. Digest. Don't copy.

Exposing yourself to great work doesn't mean imitating it. The goal isn't to repaint what you admire. It's to let it pass through you and come out changed, mixed with your history, your obsessions, your way of seeing. If the influence is still recognizable, you haven't digested it yet. Absorb the lesson, not the surface.

You are the sum of what you let in

Every image you've looked at, every book you've finished, every person you've learned from is somewhere inside your next painting. So guard the door. Let in work that makes you uncomfortable with how good it is. People who raise your standards. Ideas that take effort to understand. Close the door, gently but firmly, on what makes you smaller. You become what you look at. Look at things worth becoming. What did you let into your eyes this week?

    About

    YEKTA

    YEKTA is the name Hossein Yektapour paints under. A digital painter, he spent fifteen years as an art director making images for others before turning to his own.

    The work is conceptual and quiet: bodies that come apart, faces that turn into objects, and a red thread that keeps returning. It is the line that ties a thought to what it costs.

    overthink(paint)ing is how he names the process: thinking a feeling all the way through, until it has no choice but to become a picture.

    Alongside painting he makes interactive and generative work.
