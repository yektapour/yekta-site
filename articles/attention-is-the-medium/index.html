Attention Is the Medium.

Every artist thinks their medium is paint, or pixels, or sound, or code. I've started to believe the real medium comes before all of that. It's attention. Where it goes, the work goes. Where it leaks, the work leaks with it. And right now, there's a war for it, fought every morning, starting the second you open your eyes.

1. The first choice of the day

Every morning starts with a small fork in the road. Reach for the phone, and you start the day absorbing what other people made. Reach for the sketchbook, the canvas, a blank note, and you start the day making something yourself. Neither choice feels important at 7 a.m. But the first thing you feed your attention tends to set the tone for everything after it.

2. Make the work away from where you post it

The strongest work I've made didn't start on a platform. It started in the studio, far from any feed. Then, when it was ready, I brought it to the platform. That direction matters. Bring your real life to the feed. Be very careful about bringing the feed into your real life. When the feed starts deciding what you make, you've stopped being the artist and started being the audience.

3. Attention is expensive

Changing anything, a habit, a skill, a way of painting, costs attention. And attention is the most limited thing you own. Your brain wants to spend as little of it as possible. So the real question isn't how to get more time. It's: where is my attention best spent? Every notification, every argument in the comments, every scroll is a small withdrawal from the same account the work depends on. Budget your attention like money. Because for an artist, it's worth more.

4. Go all in on what you love

Some people's attention scatters everywhere, until they find the thing they love. Then it becomes a laser. I recognize that in myself. Give me a painting I believe in, or an interactive idea I can't stop thinking about, and hours disappear. That intensity is a gift, but only if you aim it. Whatever your thing is, don't do it halfway. Put your chips down. Be all in. The path can change over the years. What matters is that each step actually moves forward.

5. Your habits are just stories

“I'm just not a morning person.” “I'm messy, that's who I am.” “I can't finish things.” Most of us carry habits we've quietly promoted to identity. But a habit is mostly a story your nervous system keeps running by default, because it's cheap. Here's a strange exercise that works: write one page describing yourself as the opposite. The organized one. The one who finishes. You'll know it's not true yet. Read it anyway. It interrupts the old story, and an interrupted story can be rewritten. Sticky notes stop working after a day because they stop being new. A new story keeps working because it changes how you see yourself.

6. Calm and alert

The best state for creative work isn't hyped. It isn't anxious either. It's calm and alert at the same time. Before a session, try sitting still for two or three minutes, just following your breath. If you can't hold your attention on something that simple for three minutes, it's worth knowing before you ask it to hold a painting for three hours. A little stress sharpens you. Just make sure you can still be kind, and still sleep at night.

7. Big rewards without effort leave you empty

Anything that gives you a huge burst of pleasure with almost no effort comes with a hidden cost. After the spike comes a dip below where you started. Ordinary things start to feel dull. You need more just to feel normal. That's why an evening of endless scrolling can make the studio feel grey the next morning. The fix isn't more stimulation. It's less, for a while, until simple things feel good again. Reward should follow effort. When it skips the effort, it starts stealing from the work.

8. Know your sustainable pace

More isn't better. The right amount is. Figure out how much you can work, consistently, over years, not over one heroic week. Some people have huge energy. Some have less but spend it more wisely. Both can build a body of work. Find your groove. You can push maybe a little beyond it. Go too far, for too long, and you run aground. Burnout isn't a sign of dedication. It's a sign you ignored your own limits.

9. One small message, every morning

Art can be a lonely practice. Long hours, mostly alone, mostly in your own head. One of the simplest things you can do about that sounds almost silly: send someone a good morning message. Every day. No conversation needed. Just a signal that someone out there notices you're awake. Be the one who checks in. Show up for people when nothing is wrong. That's how you build the people who will show up for you when something is.

10. Turn the pain into something useful

Hard times come. Losses, failures, seasons where nothing works. The most useful thing you can do with them is transform them into something. A painting. A piece. A new direction. A decision about how to spend the time I have left. Pain that's turned into work doesn't disappear. But it stops being only pain. And gratitude doesn't make you lazy. If anything, savoring what you have gives you more energy to reach for what's next.

11. Be careful what you wish for

I'll be honest. I want my work to be seen. I want my name to travel. But fame for its own sake has a price. The more people watch you, the less freedom you have to be yourself. So I try to ask what I actually want underneath it. Work that reaches people. A few people I love who love me back. Enough to feel safe. The freedom to make what I want without being attacked for it. Those are the things that make a life rich. Recognition is welcome. It just isn't the point.

Just be there

In the end, what people remember most isn't the big events. It's the unstructured time. The hours someone simply spent with them, with no plan and no agenda. The same is true for the people in your life, and for your work. Give both your attention. Not your scheduled, optimized, half distracted attention. Your real attention. That's the medium. Everything else is just material. Where is your attention going today, and is that where you want it?

    About

    YEKTA

    YEKTA is the name Hossein Yektapour paints under. A digital painter, he spent fifteen years as an art director making images for others before turning to his own.

    The work is conceptual and quiet: bodies that come apart, faces that turn into objects, and a red thread that keeps returning. It is the line that ties a thought to what it costs.

    overthink(paint)ing is how he names the process: thinking a feeling all the way through, until it has no choice but to become a picture.

    Alongside painting he makes interactive and generative work.
