Drained Flesh: A Metamorphosis That Goes the Other Way

Drained Flesh is a digital painting I made for a collection built around one word: metamorphosis. Most of us hear that word and picture something hopeful. A caterpillar. A cocoon. Wings. I wanted to look in the other direction. What if a human being could slowly transform into something unrecognizable, and nobody could say exactly when it happened?

1. A metamorphosis that goes the other way

My interpretation of metamorphosis is about how a human being can slowly become something unrecognizable. Not a sudden monster. A gradual drift, where the line between human and something else gets harder and harder to locate. I'm interested in the strange and disturbing side of that change. For me, it's a way to explore the darker parts of being human, the parts we usually prefer not to look at for too long.

2. The man

On the left, a heavy figure leans against the wall. Eyes closed. Head dropped. Arms wrapped around his own body as if he's holding himself together. He isn't fighting anymore. He's enduring. This is the only fully human presence in the painting, and he already looks emptied out.

3. Flesh as paint

I painted him with heavy, raw strokes. Thick, uneven, almost sculpted. The skin carries colors that don't belong to healthy skin: pinks, greens, bruised purples, dull yellows. I wanted the flesh to feel alive and exhausted at the same time. Too much body, and not enough person left inside it.

4. Paint that refuses to be smooth

Up close, the surface of the figure is restless. The strokes don't settle into clean anatomy. That was deliberate. A smooth, polished body would have told the wrong story. The roughness is where the life still is. It's the evidence that this is still flesh, still breathing, even if not for much longer.

5. The mannequins

Beside him stand the opposite: mannequins. Smooth. Idealized. Perfectly calm. A human shape without a human inside it. Their surfaces are painted with control, almost polished, the exact opposite of the man's restless skin. They look more “perfect” than he does. And far less alive.

6. Falling apart, piece by piece

The further you move along the wall, the less of a body remains. A full figure. Then a detached leg. Then a head resting on a block. Read from left to right, the painting becomes a sequence: flesh, then form, then fragments. Somewhere in that sequence, the human disappears. I can't tell you exactly where. That uncertainty is the point.

7. The red thread

A red thread runs through everything. It wraps around the man, crosses into the mannequins, loops through the fragments, and spills across the floor. The red thread runs through much of my work. Here, it ties every stage of the transformation together, as if they were one body at different moments. And at the bottom of the frame, a roll unwinding on the floor, ordinary, used, discarded. I'd rather not explain every symbol. Some of them work better as questions.

8. From line to body

The process mirrored the idea. I painted the man first, fully, in heavy color. For a long time, the mannequins next to him existed only as loose lines on the wall. Watching them slowly gain weight, surface and shadow felt strange: the opposite of the story the painting tells. In the process, the empty shapes were becoming more solid, while the flesh was what the painting was draining. Every stroke on the canvas is hand painted, layer by layer.

9. The in between state

There was a moment in the process that I keep thinking about. The man finished. The mannequins still just sketches. In that state, the painting looked like the transformation itself: one fully human figure, and beside him, outlines of what he could become. Sometimes an unfinished stage tells you what the finished work is really about.

10. Butterflies over a dark metamorphosis

In the collection, the piece became a collaboration with Robin, who added procedural butterflies on top of the painting. That contrast fascinates me. The butterfly is the most familiar symbol of metamorphosis: beautiful, hopeful, light. Placed over a painting where the transformation goes the darker way, it becomes something else entirely. In Robin's words: “Yekta's piece was immediately confronting; at first I had no idea what the procedural butterflies should do over top of it. After discussing his ideas behind it, we reached a piece which I feel is truly unique in the collection. Thanks to Yekta for being a marvelous collaborator.” Thank you, Robin. Finding the right way for those two worlds to meet was the best part of this work.

Becoming unrecognizable

We tend to imagine transformation as something that happens to us all at once. I think the more frightening version is slower. A little less flesh. A little more surface. A little more like the mannequin, every year. Drained Flesh doesn't answer where the line is. It just asks you to stand in front of that wall long enough to wonder which side of it you're on. What do you see happening along that red thread?

    About

    YEKTA

    YEKTA is the name Hossein Yektapour paints under. A digital painter, he spent fifteen years as an art director making images for others before turning to his own.

    The work is conceptual and quiet: bodies that come apart, faces that turn into objects, and a red thread that keeps returning. It is the line that ties a thought to what it costs.

    overthink(paint)ing is how he names the process: thinking a feeling all the way through, until it has no choice but to become a picture.

    Alongside painting he makes interactive and generative work.
