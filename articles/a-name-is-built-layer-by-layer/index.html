An Artist's Name Is Built Like a Painting: Layer by Layer.

Copy this format. Steal this hook. Post at this hour. If you're building a name online, you're drowning in advice like that. I spent around 15 years as an art director, building brands for other people. Now I'm building one around my own work, and I'm learning that almost none of those tricks matter without a foundation underneath. Here's the foundation, the way I see it as an artist.

1. Pick the destination before you drive

If you get in a car with no destination, you can't choose a route. You can't even tell whether you're getting closer or further away. A personal brand is the same. It costs time, energy, privacy, and a lot of nerve. Without a clear reason, you'll quit the first time it gets hard. So before the next post, ask: what am I actually trying to get from this? Recognition? A gallery show? A community around the work? The answer changes everything that follows.

2. Four questions, asked backwards

Start from the end and walk back to today. One: What outcome do I want? Two: What would I need to be known for to get there? Three: What would I need to actually do to be known for that? Because you're known for what you do, not what you say. Four: What do I need to learn before I can do it? Now you have a map, and a way to measure every post: does this move me toward the outcome, or away from it?

3. Choose your pond before the ocean

Most people jump straight into the ocean. “Art.” “Design.” “Creativity.” And there they compete with people who've been doing it for decades. Start in a pond where your actual experience makes you stand out. For me, that's conceptual digital painting and interactive pieces built around it. Not “art” in general. As your experience grows, the brand can grow with it: pond, then river, then lake. But move up because your experience allows it, not because you're bored. Otherwise you're just repeating what the bigger fish already said.

4. You can't fake the receipts

Here's the uncomfortable truth. Why should anyone listen to you? Because of what you've actually done. Your work, your wins, your failures. There's no shortcut for that. Skepticism online is at an all time high. The first thing people ask is: is this person real? So collect your receipts. Finished pieces. Work that took months. Lessons that cost you something. Each one is a layer of trust.

5. Expert or student? Be honest.

There are two honest positions. The expert has done the thing and shares how. The student is in the middle of doing it, and shares the experiments and the results as they happen. Both work. Pretending to be an expert when you're a student is just lying, and people feel it. I'm both. With 15 years in art direction, I can speak about composition, concept and visual language as an expert. With interactive art and vibe coding, I'm openly a student, learning in public, piece by piece. That honesty is part of the work.

6. A brand is just a pairing, repeated

Forget logos and color palettes for a second. A brand is when people automatically connect you with something. A good brand is an intentional pairing, repeated consistently. Not once. Not on special occasions. Every time. A red thread on black. Conceptual weight. Hand painted work. The same few ideas, showing up again and again, until people think of them before they see my name. Decide what you want to be paired with. Then wear it every single day.

7. Know what you won't be paired with

What you avoid builds your brand as much as what you choose. If you want to be trusted, don't keep showing up next to people and trends that aren't. Every association becomes a lens your audience sees you through. Choose carefully, or your brand turns into a cage instead of a home.

8. Contrarian, not controversial

The strongest lever for standing out isn't a hook or a thumbnail. It's seeing your field differently from most people in it. Here's a simple exercise. On the left, write what people in your field keep saying or doing that you genuinely disagree with. On the right, write the opposite: what you believe instead. Mine: in a feed full of generated images, every painting of mine is painted by hand. And while most people use AI to skip their growth, I use it to speed mine up. Contrarian means it's a belief you can't help sharing. Controversial means you're saying it to get a reaction. Only the first one builds anything.

9. Optimize for trust, not virality

Going viral isn't bad. Chasing it is. A random clip can explode and bring you thousands of people who don't care about your actual work. Attention isn't belief. Someone can be famous and still not be trusted to deliver what you need. Virality is a spike. Trust is a staircase. Only one of them keeps you climbing.

10. Trust is a promise you keep, again and again

Here's the simplest definition I know. Trust is how much someone believes you'll meet their expectations, based on what you've done before. So you build it the obvious way: set an expectation, then meet it. Over and over. For an artist, that means: say what kind of work you make, then keep making it. Show up when you said you would. Deliver the piece the way you described it. Do that long enough, and people would have to work hard not to trust you.

Expect nothing for three years

This sounds crazy, but it's the most useful expectation I know. Go in expecting no real result for the first 36 months. Some people break through in months. Most don't. If you expect a quick win and don't get it, you quit. If you expect a long road and something happens early, it's a gift. This isn't lowering ambition. It's protecting it. The only way to win this game is to still be playing. The people who last are the ones who can keep going before anything starts paying back. A name is built like a painting. Layer by layer. Most of the layers nobody sees. What do you want people to think of when they hear your name?

    YEKTA

    YEKTA is the name Hossein Yektapour paints under. A digital painter, he spent fifteen years as an art director making images for others before turning to his own.

    The work is conceptual and quiet: bodies that come apart, faces that turn into objects, and a red thread that keeps returning. It is the line that ties a thought to what it costs.

    overthink(paint)ing is how he names the process: thinking a feeling all the way through, until it has no choice but to become a picture.

    Alongside painting he makes interactive and generative work.
