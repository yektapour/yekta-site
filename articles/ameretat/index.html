Ameretat: She Turned the Ashes into a Home for the Birds

After a forest fire, the birds come back to a world without branches. No trees. No nests. Only ash, still warm, and sparks drifting in the dark. Ameretat is a painting about what happens next. About someone who looks at what's left, and decides to build a home out of it anyway.

1. The painting

A figure stands on the right, head tilted back, eyes closed. From her head, instead of hair, a long mass of dry branches stretches across the whole canvas, like smoke caught by the wind. Inside it: nests, birds, pine cones, and a snake winding through the tangle. Below, the ground still glows red. Embers float upward into a heavy, bruised sky.

2. The name

Ameretat is an ancient Zoroastrian name. It means immortality, and it carries the idea of restoration and perseverance. Ameretat is the guardian of plants. She revives them and tries to keep them from withering. She is also the protector of animals. I didn't want to paint immortality as something untouchable or divine. I wanted to paint it as stubbornness. As the refusal to let life end where the fire stopped.

3. After the fire

Here's the story behind the image. After the forest fire, the birds had no nests left. So she dried her hair with the ashes left behind, and turned it into branches. And in those branches, she built nests, so that flight and freedom could survive. She didn't put the forest back. She became the forest.

4. The birds

In my work, birds always stand for freedom and peace. Here, they're the reason for everything. Every branch, every nest exists so that something can still fly. One bird is already in the air on the far left, leaving the shelter, or arriving at it. I like that you can't tell which. Protecting freedom sometimes means carrying its weight on your own head.

5. The snake

A snake moves through the branches and wraps around her neck. The snake stands for strength, and it also marks the negative events, the damage, the threat that never fully leaves. It lives inside the same shelter as the birds. It even touches the one holding it all together. Restoration doesn't happen in a world without danger. It happens right next to it.

6. The fire isn't over

The bottom of the painting still burns. Red sparks rise through the whole scene, even between the nests. That was important to me. This isn't a painting about after the disaster, when everything is safe again. It's about rebuilding while the ground is still hot.

7. Why a woman

I chose a female character to carry the idea of support. Someone who holds, protects and makes room for others to live, even when there is very little left to give. Her body is calm. Her face is turned upward. There's no struggle in her posture. Just the quiet decision to become a home.

8. A one by three canvas

The painting is wide: a 1:3 ratio. I chose that format to give the scene grandeur, and a sense of balance between the figure and the enormous structure growing out of her. The eye travels a long way from the bird on the left to the face on the right. That distance is part of the story. And yes, it also fits perfectly as an X banner.

9. Sketch, sculpt, paint

The piece is fully digital, built in three stages. Sketch: loose lines to find the idea and the overall form. Sculpt: the figure, the branches and the mask built in 3D, so the light and volume would feel real. Paint: everything painted over by hand, layer by layer, until the surface felt like oil on canvas. The whole process took about two weeks.

10. Where it started

It began like this. A few rough lines. At this stage, the branches are just scribbles and the mask is just an outline. But the gesture is already there: the head tilted back, the face turned toward the sky. If the sketch doesn't carry the feeling, nothing added later will save it.

11. Giving it weight

Sculpting in 3D let me test light, depth and the tangle of branches before a single color was chosen. Without color, the form has nowhere to hide. Either the structure works, or it doesn't. Then the painting began, and the cold grey forms slowly turned into skin, wood and smoke.

12. Where it lives now

Ameretat was made in a format that belongs on a profile, and today it hangs exactly there, as the banner of @LingamHero on X. There's something I love about that. A painting about building a home for others, now quietly at the top of someone else's home online. A shelter, still doing its job.

Immortality, the way I see it

We usually imagine immortality as never ending. I think the more human version is different. It's the ability to start again after the fire. To take what's left, even if it's only ash, and build something that lets life continue. That's what Ameretat means to me. Not a world that never burns. Someone who keeps building nests in it. What would you build out of the ashes?

    About

    YEKTA

    YEKTA is the name Hossein Yektapour paints under. A digital painter, he spent fifteen years as an art director making images for others before turning to his own.

    The work is conceptual and quiet: bodies that come apart, faces that turn into objects, and a red thread that keeps returning. It is the line that ties a thought to what it costs.

    overthink(paint)ing is how he names the process: thinking a feeling all the way through, until it has no choice but to become a picture.

    Alongside painting he makes interactive and generative work.
