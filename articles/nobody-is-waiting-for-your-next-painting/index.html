Nobody Is Waiting for Your Next Painting.

For about fifteen years, I worked in advertising agencies. Everything had a deadline. The pitch. The launch. The client meeting at nine tomorrow morning. Then I started putting more of my life into my own art. And I discovered something nobody warns you about. Nobody is waiting for your next painting. No client. No brief. No date on the calendar. Just you, and a canvas that will wait forever if you let it.

1. In an agency, the calendar does the pushing

Agency life runs on dates. You can drift for a while, but the presentation is coming whether you're ready or not. Somebody will walk into that room and expect to see work. That pressure is exhausting. But it's also strangely reliable. The deadline doesn't care how you feel. It just arrives, and the work arrives with it.

2. Fear is a terrible boss, and a very effective one

Most of us know the pattern. Weeks of drifting. Then a few days before the date, panic arrives, and suddenly you can work for twelve hours straight. It's not pretty. It's not healthy. But it gets the thing done. As long as there's a date, the damage is contained. The pain is short, and the work exists at the end of it.

3. There are two kinds of putting things off

The first kind has a deadline. It's loud, stressful, often funny in hindsight. And it ends. The second kind has no deadline at all. No date to trigger the panic. No moment where the fear finally forces you to start. So it doesn't end. It just quietly spreads, month after month, year after year. That's the dangerous one. And it's the one artists live with every day.

4. Art begins with an empty room

When you build something of your own, an art practice, a body of work, a new direction, there's no deadline at first. Nobody is asking for it yet. Nobody will notice if it doesn't happen this week. The momentum only comes after you've already done the hard part: made the work, shown it, made more. The cruel thing is that the stage where you most need a deadline is exactly the stage where nobody gives you one.

5. The quiet kind hurts the most

The deadline kind of procrastination is public. Everyone sees the all nighter. The no deadline kind is private. Nobody sees the painting you didn't start. The series you kept meaning to begin. The idea you've carried for three years. It doesn't feel like a crisis. It feels like nothing. The frustration isn't that you failed at your dream. It's that you never really started chasing it, and you ended up watching your own life from the side.

6. Rest that isn't rest

There's a special kind of leisure that procrastinators know well. You're scrolling, or watching something, or reorganizing a folder that didn't need organizing, while the canvas sits there. It looks like rest. It isn't. Real rest is earned and guilt free. This kind is soaked in guilt, dread and a little self disgust. You lose the work and the rest at the same time.

7. The voice that wants easy, and the one that sees far

Inside every one of us there's a part that only cares about right now: easy, comfortable, fun. And another part that can see the bigger picture: where this work could go in five years, what it could become. Sometimes they agree. Painting can be the most fun thing in the world. But often they don't, especially at the start of something hard. Without a deadline, the voice of right now wins almost every time. Not because it's stronger. Because nothing interrupts it.

8. Build your own deadlines

If nobody gives you a date, you have to make one, and make it real. An open call with a submission date. A drop you announce publicly before the piece is finished. A day of the week when something new always goes up. A deadline you only keep in your head is easy to move. A deadline someone else can see is much harder to ignore. The agency gave me deadlines for fifteen years. Now I have to be my own agency.

9. Put someone in the room

Part of what made agency deadlines work was simple: someone was going to look. You can recreate that. Share the process while it's happening. Stream a painting session. Tell a friend, a collector, another artist what you'll show them and when. Not for validation. For witness. Work that someone is expecting has a way of getting finished.

10. Count the weeks

Here's a sobering exercise. Draw one small box for every week of a long life. It fits on a single page. And a good part of that page is already used. Look at it, and ask honestly: what am I putting off that has no deadline at all? The painting. The series. The new medium you keep circling. The people you mean to call. Those things will never send you a reminder. The weeks will just keep going.

So start before anyone asks

Nobody is waiting for your next painting. That sounds sad. It's actually freedom. No client to please. No brief to follow. Nobody to tell you when it's due. Which also means nobody will tell you to start. That part is yours. Make the first mark today. Not because there's a deadline, but because there isn't one, and there never will be. What are you putting off that has no deadline at all?

    About

    YEKTA

    YEKTA is the name Hossein Yektapour paints under. A digital painter, he spent fifteen years as an art director making images for others before turning to his own.

    The work is conceptual and quiet: bodies that come apart, faces that turn into objects, and a red thread that keeps returning. It is the line that ties a thought to what it costs.

    overthink(paint)ing is how he names the process: thinking a feeling all the way through, until it has no choice but to become a picture.

    Alongside painting he makes interactive and generative work.
